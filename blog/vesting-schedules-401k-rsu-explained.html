Vesting Schedules Explained: The Deadline That Decides How Much of Your Compensation You Actually Keep

How 401(k) match and RSU vesting schedules work, the legal limits, and how much money you can lose by leaving a job too early.

If you have a 401(k) match or stock compensation, part of your pay isn't fully yours yet. It belongs to you on a schedule. Leave before that schedule finishes, and you hand the unvested part back to your employer. This is called vesting, and most people never look at it until they're already negotiating a new job offer or deciding whether to quit.

Understanding your vesting schedule is a real financial literacy gap. It can be worth tens of thousands of dollars depending on your timing, and it rarely comes up in casual conversation about pay.

What Vesting Actually Means

Vesting is the process by which money or stock your employer contributed on your behalf becomes legally, permanently yours. Until it vests, it's a promise, not an asset. If you quit or get let go before it vests, you forfeit it.

This only applies to money the employer put in. Your own contributions are never at risk.

401(k) Employer Match: The Legal Limits

Vesting schedules for 401(k) plans are governed by federal law, not just company policy. The Internal Revenue Code sets two allowable vesting timelines for employer matching and profit-sharing contributions.

Under one option, contributions must be 100% vested when a participant completes 3 years of service, commonly called three-year cliff vesting. Under the other, contributions vest gradually over 6 years, and a participant must be 100% vested upon completing 6 years of service.

Those are the slowest schedules a plan is legally allowed to use. An employer can always vest you faster, including immediately, but it can never be slower.

The standard six-year graded schedule looks like this:

Years of ServiceVested Percentage
Less than 2 years0%
2 years20%
3 years40%
4 years60%
5 years80%
6 years100%

Your own paycheck deferrals are a separate story. The money you personally contribute to your 401(k) is always fully owned by you the moment it hits the account, with no waiting period.

The Money at Stake If You Leave Early

Say you earn $80,000 a year and your employer matches 4%, or $3,200 annually. Your plan uses a three-year cliff. If you leave after two years and eleven months, every dollar of that match, plus any investment growth on it, goes back to the plan. That's roughly $9,600 in contributions alone, not counting what it would have earned invested over decades.

If you leave one month later, after hitting the three-year mark, you keep all of it. Same job, same salary, same effort, a completely different financial outcome based on a single date on a calendar.

Stock Options and RSUs: A Different Set of Rules

Restricted stock units and stock options aren't covered by the same federal vesting minimums as 401(k) plans. Companies set their own schedules, and while there's variation, one structure shows up repeatedly.

Under a standard four-year time-based vesting schedule with a one-year cliff, 1/4 of your shares vest after one year. After the cliff, 1/36 of the remaining granted shares, or 1/48 of the original grant, vest each month until the four-year vesting period is over.

That means nothing vests during your first twelve months. If you leave in month eleven, you walk away with zero shares from that grant, even if the stock has doubled in value since you were hired.

Refresh grants (additional equity awarded after your initial hire) typically come with their own new vesting clock, layered on top of whatever's left of earlier grants. This is why two employees hired the same year can have very different amounts vesting in any given year.

How to Find Your Actual Vesting Schedule

  • Pull your 401(k) Summary Plan Description, not just the enrollment brochure. It states the exact schedule and how "years of service" is counted.
  • Check your equity grant agreement for RSUs or options. The vesting start date is usually your hire date or grant date, not your first paycheck date.
  • Ask HR or your plan administrator directly for your current vested percentage in writing if the documents are unclear.
  • Recheck after any promotion, refresh grant, or plan change. New grants usually start a new vesting clock.

What to Do If You're Close to a Vesting Date

If you're job hunting and close to a cliff or a graded milestone, it's worth asking whether you can delay a start date by a few weeks. A new employer negotiating a signing bonus may be willing to help offset what you'd forfeit, but only if you know the number to ask for.

If you're the one being recruited, the reverse applies: ask what you're leaving on the table, and use that number in salary or bonus negotiations with the new employer.

Why This Belongs in Your Bigger Financial Picture

Vesting doesn't show up on a pay stub, so it's easy to forget it's part of your compensation at all. But unvested employer contributions and unvested equity are real dollars that affect your net worth timeline, your retirement savings rate, and how much cushion you actually have if you're weighing a job change. Is my 401(k) match ever vested immediately?

Yes, many employers choose immediate vesting even though it isn't required. Federal law only sets the maximum wait times: a 3-year cliff or a 6-year graded schedule. Plans are free to vest you faster, including on day one.

Are my own 401(k) contributions ever at risk of forfeiture?

No. Money you personally contribute from your paycheck is always 100% vested immediately. Vesting schedules only apply to what your employer contributes, such as matching or profit-sharing funds.

What happens to unvested RSUs if I'm laid off?

In almost all standard plans, unvested RSUs are forfeited when your employment ends, regardless of the reason, unless your specific agreement includes acceleration clauses for layoffs, which are uncommon outside of executive contracts.

Can I negotiate a better vesting schedule when I'm hired?

It's uncommon to change a 401(k) vesting schedule since it applies plan-wide to all employees, but RSU or stock option grants are sometimes negotiable, especially around cliff length or accelerated vesting upon acquisition.
