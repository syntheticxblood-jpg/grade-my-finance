Overdraft Fees: Why the $5 Cap Never Happened, and How to Avoid Paying Them Anyway

The CFPB's $5 overdraft fee cap was repealed in 2025. Here's what banks actually charge now and how to stop paying overdraft fees for good.

If you've heard that overdraft fees were capped at $5, that rule never actually took effect. Congress killed it before it could apply to a single transaction. Meanwhile, the fee for spending a few dollars more than you have in checking is still, on average, close to $27 — and can run as high as $35 at some of the largest banks. This article covers what actually happened to the overdraft rule, what banks charge today, and the specific steps that stop these fees for good.

What triggers an overdraft fee

An overdraft fee is charged when your bank allows a debit card purchase, check, or automatic payment to go through even though you don't have enough money in your account, and then bills you for covering the gap. Instead of declining the transaction, the bank pays it and charges you for the privilege — usually a flat fee regardless of whether you were $2 or $200 short.

A related but separate charge is the non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee, which hits when the bank declines the transaction outright rather than covering it. Some banks charge both types; a growing number have dropped NSF fees specifically while keeping standard overdraft fees in place.

What happened to the $5 overdraft fee cap

In December 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalized a rule that would have required banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets to cap overdraft fees at $5, set the fee at an amount that covers actual costs, or disclose overdrafts as credit subject to the Truth in Lending Act. The rule was scheduled to take effect October 1, 2025.

It never got there. Congress overturned the rule using the fast-track procedures of the Congressional Review Act through the passage of S.J.Res. 18, which the President signed into law (P.L. 119-10). On May 9, 2025, President Trump signed into law a measure eliminating the CFPB's Overdraft Rule, which applied to banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets and offered these institutions several options to manage their overdraft lending program.

The repeal was permanent in a specific legal sense: the CFPB's overdraft rule is now void, and the Bureau is barred from issuing a substantially similar regulation in the future under the Congressional Review Act. That means the $5 cap isn't coming back through this same rule, and there is no federal price ceiling on what a bank can charge you for an overdraft today. Large depository institutions are no longer subject to federal overdraft fee caps, though they should remain attentive to state-level regulatory activity concerning fees.

What banks actually charge now

Without a federal cap, overdraft pricing varies a lot by bank. According to industry surveys conducted after the repeal:

Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study found the average overdraft fee fell 1% year-over-year to $26.77, while the average non-sufficient funds fee declined for the fourth consecutive year to a record-low $16.82. At the same time, the national average overdraft fee sits at $26.77 per transaction, but at the largest banks the median is closer to $35.

Some banks have moved away from overdraft fees entirely as a way to compete for customers and avoid regulatory scrutiny. Capital One, Citibank, and Ally Bank have all dropped overdraft fees completely. If your bank still charges $30-plus every time you dip below zero by even a few dollars, that's a real signal to compare what else is out there — the same way you'd shop for a better rate on a high-yield savings account.

Why this fee is so expensive relative to what it "buys" you

Overdraft fees function like a very short-term loan, and the effective cost is steep. The CFPB found that among transactions that triggered an overdraft, the median size was $50 — and if you overdraft your account by $50 and pay the average overdraft fee of $25.20, that's the equivalent of paying 50.4% to borrow the money for a few days. Annualize that and the rate would be far worse than even the priciest credit card. A $4 coffee that triggers a $35 fee is a $39 coffee — and if two more small transactions clear before you notice, you could be looking at over $100 in fees for what started as a minor timing gap.

How to actually stop paying overdraft fees

  • Opt out of overdraft coverage on debit card transactions. Opting out of overdraft coverage under Regulation E is the simplest way to avoid debit card overdraft fees — transactions are declined instead of approved and charged. A declined purchase is embarrassing for a second; a $35 fee follows you around for the rest of the month.
  • Link a savings account as backup. Most banks offer automatic transfers from savings to checking when you're about to go negative, usually for a small flat fee (often $0–$10) instead of a full overdraft charge.
  • Turn on low-balance alerts. Set a text or push notification for when your checking balance drops below a threshold — $100 or $200 — so you can move money before a payment clears.
  • Consider switching banks if you overdraft often. If you're a frequent overdrafter, moving to a bank that charges $0 for overdrafts isn't a minor convenience — it can save you hundreds of dollars a year in fees alone.
  • Build a small buffer. Even $200–$300 sitting in checking as a cushion, separate from your emergency fund, absorbs the timing mismatches (paycheck lands Friday, rent debits Thursday) that cause most accidental overdrafts.

None of these steps require a subscription, an app, or a new financial habit you have to maintain forever — they're mostly one-time settings. The bottom line

The $5 overdraft cap made headlines, got finalized, and then got repealed before it ever applied to a single transaction. Overdraft fees today look almost exactly like they did before the rule existed — averaging around $27, with some big banks still charging $34 to $35. The fix isn't waiting on future regulation; it's opting out of overdraft coverage, linking a backup account, and setting balance alerts so the fee never has a chance to trigger in the first place.

Is there still a federal cap on overdraft fees?

No. The CFPB finalized a rule in December 2024 that would have capped overdraft fees at large banks at $5, but Congress repealed it under the Congressional Review Act and President Trump signed the repeal into law on May 9, 2025. The rule never took effect, and the CFPB is barred from issuing a similar rule again.

How much do banks typically charge for an overdraft now?

According to Bankrate's 2025 checking account survey, the national average overdraft fee is about $26.77 per transaction, though many large banks charge $34 to $35. Some banks, including several online banks, charge nothing at all.

What's the easiest way to stop paying overdraft fees?

Opt out of overdraft coverage on your debit card so transactions are simply declined instead of approved and charged a fee. You can also link a savings account for automatic transfers and turn on low-balance alerts.

Is an overdraft fee the same as a non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee?

No. An overdraft fee is charged when the bank covers a transaction that overdraws your account. An NSF fee is charged when the bank instead declines the transaction. Many banks have eliminated NSF fees while still charging standard overdraft fees.
