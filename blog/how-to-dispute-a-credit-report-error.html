How to Dispute an Error on Your Credit Report (Step by Step)

A step-by-step guide to disputing credit report errors with Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion, including timelines and what to do if you're denied.

Your credit report is basically a permanent record that lenders, landlords, and sometimes employers use to decide whether they trust you with money. If it has a mistake on it — an account that isn't yours, a late payment that was actually on time, a balance that's wrong — that mistake can cost you a lower credit limit, a higher interest rate, or a denied application. And errors are more common than most people assume.

A Federal Trade Commission study of the U.S. credit reporting industry found that five percent of consumers had errors on one of their three major credit reports that could lead to them paying more for products such as auto loans and insurance, and one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their three credit reports. That's not a rare edge case. That's tens of millions of people. If you've never actually pulled your own report and read it line by line, there's a real chance something on it is wrong.

Step 1: Get your actual credit reports, not just your score

Your credit score is a summary number. The report is the underlying data that number is built from, and it's the report — not the score — that you dispute. The three major U.S. credit reporting agencies — Equifax, Experian and TransUnion — offer free weekly credit reports to consumers on AnnualCreditReport.com. This became a permanent benefit, not a temporary pandemic perk. The free weekly reports began in April 2020 at the start of the COVID-19 crisis, and the service was initially in place for a year, but it became permanent in September 2023.

Pull all three reports — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — because they don't always contain the same information. A lender might report to only one or two bureaus, so an error on your Equifax file might not show up on TransUnion at all.

Step 2: Actually read the report, line by line

Skimming doesn't catch errors. Go through every account and check:

  • Accounts you don't recognize (a sign of identity theft or a mixed file)
  • Late payments listed on accounts you paid on time
  • Balances that don't match your actual statements
  • Accounts marked "open" that you closed years ago
  • Debts that should have aged off after seven years but are still showing
  • Your name, address, or employer information mixed up with someone else's

Write down exactly what's wrong and why. Vague disputes get rejected faster than specific ones with evidence attached.

Step 3: File the dispute — in writing, with documentation

If you identify an error on your credit report, you should start by disputing that information with the credit reporting company, explaining in writing what you think is wrong, why, and including copies of documents that support your dispute. All three bureaus let you file disputes online, but a written paper trail (even if you use their online form) gives you a clearer record if things go sideways later.

Fixing an error generally means contacting both the credit reporting company and the company that provided the information. That second part matters — you can dispute directly with your bank or the collection agency too, not just the bureau. Doing both increases your odds and creates two separate paper trails.

Step 4: Know the timeline you're entitled to

If you dispute an error on your credit report, a credit reporting company generally must investigate the dispute within 30 days of receiving it, and they have five business days after completing an investigation to notify you of the results. There are some cases in which the company may take up to 45 days: if you file a dispute after receiving your free annual credit report, or if you submit additional information relevant to your dispute during the 30-day window.

If your dispute gets tossed out, the bureau doesn't get to just ignore you. If the credit bureau considers your request to be frivolous or irrelevant, they will stop investigating, but they need to notify you of that and give the reason, and you may need to give them additional evidence to support your request.

Step 5: What happens if you're right

If the investigation shows the furnisher provided wrong information about you, or the information cannot be verified, the furnisher must update or remove the information and notify all the credit reporting companies after correcting it, and then the credit reporting companies must update your credit reports. You should get a written notice of the outcome along with an updated copy of your report.

Step 6: What happens if they say you're wrong

Sometimes the investigation comes back and the bureau sides with the furnisher. You still have options. You have the right to add a statement to your credit file, and you can ask that the statement be included on your future credit reports — this only applies to disputes you have submitted with the credit reporting agency. It won't remove the item, but it does give future lenders your side of the story.

If you believe the bureau or furnisher mishandled the process entirely, you can escalate. If you have a problem with your credit report, you can also submit a complaint with the CFPB. Just know the CFPB expects you to try the direct dispute route first — you shouldn't submit a complaint about inaccurate or incomplete information while your dispute with the relevant credit or consumer reporting agency remains active, since credit and consumer reporting agencies are required to respond to disputes within 30-45 days.

What to avoid

Don't dispute accurate information just to see if it sticks. It wastes your time and can flag you as a serial disputer. And you don't need to pay anyone to do this for you — the dispute process is free and anyone can do it themselves by writing directly to the bureaus and furnishers.

Also, don't confuse this process with identity theft recovery. If accounts on your report were opened fraudulently in your name, that's a different and more urgent process — start at IdentityTheft.gov instead of the standard dispute path.

Why this is worth an afternoon of your time

A single incorrect late payment or an account that isn't yours can knock points off your score, which can mean a higher interest rate on a car loan or mortgage — often worth thousands of dollars over the life of the loan. Checking your reports for free every week and fixing what's wrong costs you nothing but time. It's one of the highest-leverage financial chores you can do in a single sitting. If you're not sure where your credit stands relative to everything else — debt, savings, income — running a quick check of your financial grade can help you see where a credit report fix would move the needle most.

Quick recap

  • Pull all three reports free, every week, at AnnualCreditReport.com
  • Read every line — errors are common, not rare
  • Dispute in writing with both the bureau and the furnisher
  • Expect a response within 30 to 45 days
  • If denied, add a statement to your file or escalate to the CFPB

How often can I check my credit report for free?

You can check your report from all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — once per week, for free, permanently, at AnnualCreditReport.com. This became a permanent program after starting as a pandemic-era measure.

How long does a credit report dispute take?

Generally 30 days for the bureau to investigate, though it can extend to 45 days in certain cases, such as if you submit new information partway through the review or filed the dispute right after requesting your free annual report.

Does disputing an error hurt my credit score?

No. Filing a dispute itself does not affect your score. If the dispute results in a correction, your score may actually improve.

What if the credit bureau says my dispute is 'frivolous'?

They must notify you within five business days and explain why, often asking for more supporting documentation. You can resubmit with additional evidence.

Do I need to pay a credit repair company to dispute an error?

No. The dispute process is free and you can do everything a credit repair company does yourself by contacting the bureaus and furnishers directly.
