Your Freedom Number: What It Is and How to Calculate It

Your freedom number is the amount of money that would let your investments cover your living expenses indefinitely, without needing to work. It's a simpler idea than it sounds — and the assumption behind it matters more than the final figure.

The core formula

Your freedom number is your annual spending divided by an assumed safe withdrawal rate. At a commonly used 4% rate, that means multiplying annual spending by 25. Someone spending $50,000 a year would have a freedom number around $1.25 million.

Why the withdrawal rate is the number that actually matters

The withdrawal rate assumption drives everything. A more conservative 3% assumption pushes that same $50,000/year target up to about $1.67 million; a more aggressive 5% brings it down to $1 million. Small changes in that one assumption move your real target by hundreds of thousands of dollars — which is why understanding it matters more than memorizing the "multiply by 25" shortcut.

It's not the same as a traditional retirement number

Traditional retirement planning often targets replacing 70-80% of your working income. A freedom number is different — it's built directly from your actual spending, not a percentage of past income, which makes it a more personal, more accurate target if your retirement lifestyle doesn't closely track your working-years income.

Why it's worth tracking as a percentage, not just a dollar figure

"I'm 40% of the way to my freedom number" is a far more useful thing to track over time than the raw dollar target alone — it turns an abstract, far-off goal into visible, ongoing progress you can actually watch move.

What to do once you know it

The number itself doesn't change your finances — it gives you a real target to measure progress against, and a way to see concretely how changes (a raise, cutting a recurring expense, an extra year of saving) move your actual timeline, instead of guessing.

Frequently asked questions

Is a freedom number the same thing as 'FIRE'?

It's the underlying calculation behind the FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) movement, but the concept applies to anyone planning eventual financial independence, not just people pursuing early retirement specifically.

Should I use my current spending or my expected retirement spending?

Ideally your expected future spending, since that's what your investments will actually need to cover — though current spending is a reasonable, more knowable starting estimate if future spending is hard to predict.
