Home Buying

How Much House Can I Actually Afford?

A lender will approve you for more than you should actually spend. Here's the more realistic version of the question.

The common lender-based rule: 28/36

Many conventional lenders use a guideline where your housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance) shouldn't exceed roughly 28% of gross monthly income, and total debt payments (housing plus everything else) shouldn't exceed roughly 36%. This is a lending guideline, not necessarily a comfortable-living guideline.

Why "approved for" and "comfortable with" are different numbers

Lenders calculate what you can technically repay based on income and existing debt — they don't account for how much you actually want to save, how variable your other expenses are, or your personal risk tolerance for a large fixed payment. Plenty of people who get approved for a certain amount later feel "house poor" — technically able to make payments, but with little room left for anything else.

A more conservative framework some planners suggest

ApproachRough guideline
Lender max (28/36 rule)Up to ~28% of gross income on housing
More conservative planning guideline20–25% of take-home (after-tax) pay
Total home price vs. income (rough multiple)Often cited around 2.5–3.5× annual gross income

These are rough anchors, not universal rules — cost of living, interest rates, and personal priorities all shift what's actually reasonable.

Don't forget the costs beyond the mortgage payment

  • Property taxes and homeowners insurance (often bundled into the payment, but still a real cost)
  • Maintenance — commonly estimated around 1% of home value per year, though this varies a lot by home age/condition
  • HOA fees, if applicable
  • Closing costs upfront (typically 2-5% of purchase price)
  • The opportunity cost of the down payment itself — money that's no longer liquid or invested elsewhere

The emergency fund question this raises

A house is illiquid — you can't easily access equity in an emergency the way you could with cash savings. Most guidance suggests having a full emergency fund in place before stretching for a home purchase, not after, since a major repair or income disruption right after buying is a genuinely common source of financial stress.

Frequently asked questions

Should I max out what a lender approves me for?

Not necessarily — lender approval reflects what you can technically repay, not necessarily what leaves comfortable room for savings, emergencies, and other goals. Many financial planners suggest staying meaningfully under the maximum approved amount.

Is 20% down payment required?

No — many loan programs allow much lower down payments, though below 20% often triggers private mortgage insurance (PMI), an added monthly cost worth factoring into affordability calculations.
